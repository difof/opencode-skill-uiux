# Art direction for a real screen

First name the audience, the job, and the content that earns top billing. Inspect the current brand, type, layout, and nearby screens. A marketing page may justify expressive imagery; an operator comparing incidents needs reliable alignment and scanability. Do not make a bug fix an excuse to replace either identity.

## From brief to composition

1. Write one sentence that names the intended result. For example: “A returning visitor should see how this tool differs and reach the live demo without scrolling.” Rank the first three things the eye should find.
2. Choose a hierarchy and define its behavior at narrow widths. Use shared edges, whitespace, and typographic contrast before adding containers. A single distinctive element grounded in the subject beats unrelated gradients, badges, or stock card grids.
3. Reuse the product’s type and semantic colors. For a new system, define roles for heading, body, label, metadata, number, canvas, surface, action, and focus. Check actual contrast; choose readable measures and avoid shrinking labels to fit.
4. Design with plausible content, including long labels, unavailable data, and a narrow viewport. Do not invent customer quotes, logos, metrics, or product capabilities as visual filler.
5. Inspect the actual rendering when available. Can the primary job be identified? Does the next action look actionable before hover? What can be removed without losing meaning? Adjust the most consequential issue first.

For major redesigns, give a short rationale and one or two genuinely different directions when requested. For an existing screen, preserve familiar navigation and controls unless a specific problem warrants moving them. Separate aesthetic choices from accessibility and functional requirements.
